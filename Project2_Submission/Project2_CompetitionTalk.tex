%==============================================================
\begin{frame}{Same problem, new rule}
The physics from Project 1 hasn't changed. We still have a hidden
$28 \times 28$ digit, a list of 1900 boundary voltage readings, and
the forward simulator $F$. The job is still to work backward from the
readings to the digit.

\vspace{0.6em}
What changed is the rule on the prior. In Project 1 we used the 60{,}000
MNIST training images as a lookup table. For Project 2 the prior has to
be a diffusion model. A diffusion model is a neural network that learned
what a digit looks like by practicing on the MNIST set. We can ask it to
draw a fresh digit, but it does not know about our voltage readings.

\vspace{0.6em}
The rest of this talk is how we combined a diffusion model with the ERT
measurements. The final pipeline uses a class-conditional diffusion
prior with classifier-free guidance and a total-variation polish. The
final misfit is
\textcolor{osuorange}{$\mathbf{1.83 \times 10^{-11}}$}, recovering a
clean tilted 5.
\end{frame}

%==============================================================
\begin{frame}{What a diffusion model lets us do}
\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.55\textwidth}
\vspace{0.4em}
{\small A diffusion model is trained to look at a noisy image and predict
the noise that was added. Once it can do that, simple algebra gives us a
guess at the clean image underneath:}
\[
\hat x_0 \;=\;
\frac{x_t \,-\, \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}.
\]
\vspace{-0.3em}

{\small So at any moment along a noisy chain, the network gives us its
best guess at the clean digit underneath. At high noise the guess is
fuzzy. At low noise it is sharp. Every method here leans on that one
identity.}

\column{0.45\textwidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig05_tweedie_strip.png}\\
{\scriptsize\color{osugray} Top: a noisy input at four noise levels.
Bottom: the network's clean-image guess at each.}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%==============================================================
\begin{frame}{What we tried first, and why none of it worked}
\footnotesize
\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.58\textwidth}

We ran four standard diffusion-based recipes before landing on the
method in this talk. Each one steers a diffusion model with the ERT
measurements in a different way.

\vspace{0.3em}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep=2pt
\item \textbf{DPS} (Diffusion Posterior Sampling). At every denoising
  step, nudge the image in the direction of the data gradient
  $\nabla \log p(y\mid x)$. \textit{Result: 4 of 5 chains converged to
  a 4. Wrong digit every time.}
\item \textbf{DAPS} (Decoupled Annealing Posterior Sampling). The
  diffusion model is responsible for class and shape; a separate
  Langevin step is responsible for fitting the data. \textit{Result:
  still a 4 most runs; a single 5 only when seeded carefully.}
\item \textbf{DI-RTG v1} (DAPS warm-start + an MNIST classifier vote +
  Levenberg-Marquardt polish). The classifier was supposed to break
  the ambiguity. \textit{Result: classifier voted ``4'' too. Polish
  drove a wrong-digit answer to high precision.}
\item \textbf{DI-RTG v2} (DAPS warm-start + template retrieval from
  the MNIST training set). \textit{Result: the closest template was
  always upright, but the competition digit is tilted by 7.5 degrees.
  Retrieved templates were the right digit but at the wrong pose, and
  the polish could not unfold them.}
\end{itemize}

\column{0.42\textwidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{dps_chains.png}\\
{\scriptsize\color{osugray} The DPS run that started it all. Eight
chains, each locking onto a wrong digit (3, 4, 6, 8). Not a 5 in sight.}

\vspace{0.6em}
\begin{center}
{\footnotesize\textcolor{osuorange}{\textbf{Pattern.}}}\\
{\scriptsize\color{osugray}
Every recipe that nudges the diffusion chain by a weak data gradient at
each step commits to whichever upright digit shape is closest to the
data, then refuses to leave that basin. The basin happened to be a 4.}
\end{center}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%==============================================================
\begin{frame}{Why those four approaches all failed in the same way}
\footnotesize
Two things broke at once, and every standard recipe inherits both
problems.

\vspace{0.5em}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep=4pt
\item The little nudge at each step is in the direction of the data
  gradient. That gradient is dominated by directions the boundary
  sensors hardly see, so most of the nudge goes to waste. The piece of
  the gradient that actually moves the digit toward the data is tiny.
\item The denoising process only has about 100 steps. A hundred weak
  nudges are not enough to override the prior's pull toward the most
  common-looking upright digit.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.5em}
The chain commits early to whatever upright digit shape is closest to
the data, then spends the remaining steps polishing that wrong digit.
DAPS already decouples the prior step from the data step, but its data
step is still a sequence of small Langevin gradient updates, so it
inherits the same weakness. The classifier and the template retrieval
both run on top of a broken first stage, so they inherit its wrong
digit.

\vspace{0.5em}
The fix has to give the data step real teeth: instead of a small
gradient nudge at every iteration, solve a small optimization problem
that uses the full Jacobian. That lets one update do the work of dozens
of gradient steps and gives the data enough leverage to push the chain
out of the wrong basin.
\end{frame}

%==============================================================
\begin{frame}{The fix: split the work between two variables}
\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.55\textwidth}
\vspace{0.2em}
\footnotesize
DPS asks one variable to do two jobs at once: look like a digit, and
fit the data. The two pulls compromise on every step, and the smaller
one loses.

\vspace{0.5em}
Instead, introduce a second variable. Call them $x$ and $z$. Give them
separate jobs:

\vspace{0.3em}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep=3pt
\item $x$ only has to look like a digit.
\item $z$ only has to fit the measurements.
\item A soft penalty keeps $z$ near $x$.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.5em}
Then alternate. Update $z$ to fit the data. Update $x$ to look like a
digit. Repeat. Each update does one thing well.

\vspace{0.4em}
This sampler is called PnP-DM (Wu et al., NeurIPS 2024). We run 80
alternations per chain, 32 chains in parallel.

%==============================================================
\begin{frame}{What the data step actually minimizes}
\footnotesize
The data step picks the $z$ that does two things at once: fits the
measurements, and stays close to the current $x$. Written as an
objective:
\[
\mathcal{L}(z)
\;=\;
\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2\sigma_{\text{meas}}^2}\,\norm{y_{\text{obs}} - F(\sigma_{\text{bg}} + z)}^2}_{\textcolor{osuorange}{\text{data misfit}}}
\;\;+\;\;
\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2\sigma_n^2}\,\norm{z - x}^2}_{\textcolor{osuorange}{\text{coupling penalty}}}.
\]
\vspace{-0.3em}

\textbf{Piece by piece.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep=2pt
\item $y_{\text{obs}}$ is the 1900 measured voltages.
$F(\sigma_{\text{bg}} + z)$ is what the simulator would predict for our
candidate. The squared norm is the sum of per-electrode errors.
\item $\sigma_{\text{meas}}$ is how noisy we think the measurements
are. Smaller $\sigma_{\text{meas}}$ means we trust them more, so the
first term gets a bigger weight.
\item $\norm{z - x}^2$ pulls $z$ back toward the current $x$. The
weight $1/\sigma_n^2$ depends on the slack: tight slack means strong
pull.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Reading it.} ``Find a $z$ that explains the measurements, but
do not stray too far from the current digit guess.'' At the start of
the chain $\sigma_n$ is big (loose leash), so the data wins. As the
schedule anneals, $\sigma_n$ shrinks and the coupling wins, forcing
$z$ and $x$ to agree.
\end{frame}

%==============================================================
\begin{frame}{How we solve it: one Gauss-Newton step}
\footnotesize
DPS takes a small step in the gradient direction. We instead linearize
$F$ around the current $z$, plug the linearization into $\mathcal{L}$,
and minimize the resulting quadratic. The minimum is the solution of a
small linear system in the update $\Delta$:
\[
\underbrace{\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\sigma_{\text{meas}}^2} J^\top J + \tfrac{1}{\sigma_n^2} I\Bigr)}_{\textcolor{osuorange}{\text{curvature, with damping}}}
\;\Delta
\;=\;
\underbrace{-\tfrac{1}{\sigma_{\text{meas}}^2} J^\top r}_{\textcolor{osuorange}{\text{data pull}}}
\;+\;
\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{\sigma_n^2}(x - z)}_{\textcolor{osuorange}{\text{coupling pull}}}.
\]
\vspace{-0.3em}

\textbf{Piece by piece.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep=2pt
\item $\Delta$ is the update we are solving for. After solving:
  $z \leftarrow z + \Delta$.
\item $J^\top J$ is the curvature of the data term. In directions
  where $J$ has big columns (sensitive pixels), the curvature is big
  and the step is small. The $\sigma_n^{-2} I$ damping fills in the
  directions $J$ cannot see, so the matrix is always invertible.
\item $r = F(\sigma_{\text{bg}} + z) - y_{\text{obs}}$ is the current
  residual. Multiplying by $J^\top$ back-projects it onto pixels:
  ``which pixels would reduce this residual.''
\item $(x - z)$ is the coupling restoring force: pulls $z$ back toward
  the current $x$.
\end{itemize}

\centering
\textcolor{osuorange}{One Gauss-Newton step does the work of dozens of gradient steps on ill-conditioned $J$.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where each chain starts, and why answers can disagree}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Where does a chain start?} Not from the measurements. Each
chain starts from a fresh draw of pure Gaussian noise:
$x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$. That is just a $28 \times 28$ grid of
random numbers (TV static). The measurements $y_{\text{obs}}$ enter
only through the data step, which runs at every iteration. The data
step pulls toward fitting $y_{\text{obs}}$. The prior step pulls
toward looking like a digit. Together they convert the random noise
into the recovered digit.

\vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Why several different chains can give different digits.} The
forward map is a $784 \to 1900$ blur. Two different digits can
produce voltage readings that agree to many decimal places. So the
question ``which digit is inside?'' does not have a unique answer
from the readings alone. The posterior has \emph{several} valleys,
one per plausible digit.

\vspace{0.5em}
A single chain commits to whichever valley its random starting noise
rolls it into. Different chains, different starting noises, different
valleys. With the base PnP-DM sampler, across 32 chains most land on
the 5 valley (the correct one for this $y_{\text{obs}}$), a few land on
the 8 valley. We pick the chain with the lowest data misfit. The 5
wins every time.

\vspace{0.4em}
\centering
\textcolor{osuorange}{The 32 chains are a safety net.
The lowest-misfit chain wins the vote.}

\vspace{0.2em}
{\scriptsize\color{osugray}
This is also why one held-out test (digit 3) recovers as a 5. That
particular 3 and a particular 5 produce nearly identical voltage
readings, so no method can tell them apart from the data alone. The
class-conditional upgrade later in this talk gives the chain a way
to commit to a class hypothesis directly, instead of waiting for the
data step to roll it into a valley.}
\end{frame}

%==============================================================
\begin{frame}{One more piece: rotation augmentation}
\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.55\textwidth}
\vspace{0.2em}
\footnotesize
The pretrained MNIST diffusion model has only seen upright digits
during training. Our truth is a 5 tilted by about $7.5$ degrees. During
PnP-DM the prior step keeps nudging back toward upright, while the data
step pulls toward tilted. Without intervention the prior wins.

\vspace{0.5em}
The fix is to train the diffusion model on rotated copies of the MNIST
digits. For the base prior we fine-tune the pretrained checkpoint for
five epochs ($\sim 20$ minutes on a MacBook). For the new prior we
retrain from scratch with rotation augmentation for 30 epochs ($\sim
15$ minutes on a 5090).

\vspace{0.5em}
After this training, the model thinks tilted digits are just as normal
as upright ones. The prior step stops fighting the data, and the chain
converges to the truth.

\begin{frame}{Finishing the job: a Newton-style polish}
\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.50\textwidth}
\vspace{0.1em}
\footnotesize
After PnP-DM the best chain has misfit about $1.8 \times 10^{-7}$.
We know the digit is right. The rest is a local optimization on
the pixels.

\vspace{0.4em}
Project 1's polish was gradient descent. It converges linearly, and
the steps get tinier as we approach the optimum.

\vspace{0.4em}
We replace it with a Newton-style step that accounts for the curvature
of the data-fit landscape. Each step roughly squares the error, so a
handful of steps drops the misfit many orders of magnitude.

\vspace{0.3em}
With this polish the \textbf{base pipeline} (rotation-aug DDPM +
PnP-DM + Newton polish) ends at misfit about
$\mathbf{5 \times 10^{-11}}$.

\begin{frame}{The base pipeline got a 5, but the image had speckle}
\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.50\textwidth}
\vspace{0.1em}
\footnotesize
The base pipeline (everything we have shown so far) recovers a 5 at
misfit around $5 \times 10^{-11}$. That is already much tighter than
Project 1's $10^{-7}$, but the image had two visible problems.

\vspace{0.4em}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep=3pt
\item \textbf{Stroke speckle.} Inside the 5 there are pixels darker
  than they should be. The polish drove the data fit so hard it
  picked up the round-off noise of the simulator.
\item \textbf{Background haze.} The pixels outside the 5 are not
  perfectly background. Same cause.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.4em}
Lower misfit is not the same as a cleaner image. We needed to fix the
image without throwing away the data fit.

%==============================================================
\begin{frame}{Three improvements we layered on top of PnP-DM}
\footnotesize
\begin{itemize}\itemsep=5pt
\item \textbf{Improvement 1 --- TV-regularized polish.} Add a smoothed
  total-variation penalty to the polish objective so the polish cannot
  introduce per-pixel speckle even when it has the freedom to. Same
  Gauss-Newton step, plus a small TV gradient step every iteration.
\item \textbf{Improvement 2 --- bigger prior with EMA.} Train a
  6-million-parameter UNet from scratch on rotated MNIST, with an
  exponential-moving-average copy of the weights. EMA gives smoother
  samples; the bigger model has more capacity to represent slanted
  stroke styles.
\item \textbf{Improvement 3 --- class-conditional prior with
  classifier-free guidance.} Train the UNet with the digit label as an
  extra input. At each denoising step, combine the conditional and
  unconditional predictions: $\varepsilon = (1+w)\varepsilon_c -
  w\,\varepsilon_u$ with $w=3$. Each chain commits to a digit class
  early and stays there.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.5em}
{\color{osugray}
The first improvement changes the polish. The second and third change
the prior. The data step (Gauss-Newton on the simulator) does not change.}
\end{frame}

%==============================================================
\begin{frame}{Class-conditional prior + classifier-free guidance}
\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.50\textwidth}
\footnotesize
For each class label $c \in \{0,1,\dots,9\}$ the trained UNet learns
its own conditional distribution. During training, 10\% of the labels
are replaced with a special ``unconditional'' label so the same
network also learns the marginal distribution over all digits.

\vspace{0.4em}
At inference we combine the two predictions:
\[
\varepsilon_{\text{guided}}
\;=\;
(1+w)\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c)
\;-\;
w\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t, \varnothing).
\]
With $w=3$, samples are strongly class-faithful. We assign each of
the 32 chains a target class label that cycles through $0\dots9$,
so every digit gets at least three chains. The chain whose final
misfit is lowest wins.

\vspace{0.4em}
This is the standard CFG recipe (Ho and Salimans 2022). It gives the
sampler the option to commit to a digit \emph{early}, instead of
waiting to be pushed there by the data gradient.

\column{0.50\textwidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig16_cond_samples.png}\\[0.2em]
{\scriptsize\color{osugray} One unconditional sample per class from the
trained model, $w=3$. Each digit is rendered in its own style.}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%==============================================================
\begin{frame}{Total-variation polish: keep the misfit, drop the speckle}
\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.55\textwidth}
\footnotesize
The Newton-style polish minimizes
$\tfrac{1}{2}\norm{F(\sigma_{\text{bg}}+x) - y_{\text{obs}}}^2$. The TV
polish adds a smoothed total-variation penalty:
\[
\tfrac{1}{2}\norm{F(\sigma_{\text{bg}}+x) - y_{\text{obs}}}^2
\;+\;
\tau \sum_{i,j} \sqrt{(\partial_x x)^2 + (\partial_y x)^2 + \epsilon^2}.
\]
\vspace{-0.4em}

The second term counts the total horizontal and vertical pixel-to-pixel
variation. Speckle adds a lot of variation; smooth strokes add little.
At $\tau \approx 10^{-5}$ the TV term is small enough that the misfit
still drops to the $10^{-11}$ regime, but large enough that the polish
is no longer allowed to add per-pixel noise.

\vspace{0.5em}
Each polish iteration is one Newton step on the data term followed by
one gradient step on the TV term. The line search still gates updates,
so the misfit never increases.

\column{0.45\textwidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig15_tv_vs_lm.png}\\[0.1em]
{\scriptsize\color{osugray} The TV polish keeps the strokes clean and
the background flat while still hitting low misfit.}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The 12-combination sweep}
\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.42\textwidth}
\footnotesize
We tested all $3 \times 2 \times 2 = 12$ combinations:

\vspace{0.4em}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep=2pt
\item \textbf{Prior:} base / bigger+EMA / class-conditional+CFG
\item \textbf{Schedule:} geomspace / DAPS++ negative-rho
\item \textbf{Polish:} Newton / TV-regularized
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.5em}
Two things popped out.

\vspace{0.3em}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep=2pt
\item \textbf{TV polish beat Newton polish in every prior-schedule pair.}
  Uniformly lower misfit \emph{and} cleaner image.
\item \textbf{The class-conditional prior plus the geomspace schedule
  plus the TV polish was the lowest misfit overall.} $1.83 \times
  10^{-11}$, with a clean recognizable 5.

\begin{frame}{Summary}
\footnotesize
\begin{itemize}\itemsep=4pt
\item Four standard diffusion-based recipes (DPS, DAPS, classifier-vote,
  template retrieval) all committed to the wrong digit because their
  data step is too weak.
\item Variable splitting separates the two jobs and the Gauss-Newton
  solve gives the data step real teeth, so it can push the chain out
  of a wrong-digit basin. 32 parallel chains cover the posterior modes.
\item Rotation augmentation is a one-time fix that unlocks the
  diffusion prior for tilted digits.
\item On top of the base PnP-DM sampler we layered three improvements:
  TV-regularized polish, a bigger DDPM trained with EMA, and a
  class-conditional DDPM with classifier-free guidance. We swept all
  $12$ combinations to find the winner.
\item \textbf{Result:} digit \textbf{5}, final misfit
  \textcolor{osuorange}{$\mathbf{1.83 \times 10^{-11}}$}
  (about $5{,}400 \times$ tighter than Project 1's
  rotation-aware result), with clean strokes and a flat background.
  Base pipeline held-out: \textbf{9 out of 10}.
\end{itemize}
